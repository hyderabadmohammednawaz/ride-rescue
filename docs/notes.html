01 The problem it solves

Imagine you are riding your scooter and it breaks down at the roadside. Today you would have to ring around for a mechanic's number, describe where you are badly over the phone, wait with no idea whether anyone is coming, and argue about the price afterwards.

RideRescue turns that into one button. You press it, the app finds the nearest available mechanic automatically, and you watch them travel towards you on a map — the way you watch a food delivery.

It does two other things as well: you can book a service in advance rather than as an emergency, and you can buy spare parts from an online shop that delivers to you.

That is the whole product. Everything else here is how it works.

02 How an app like this works

This is the most important idea in the project. Understand this part and the rest is detail.

Think of a restaurant:

RestaurantSoftwareWhat it means
The dining roomFrontendWhat the customer sees and touches
The kitchenBackendWhere the real work happens, out of sight
The pantryDatabaseWhere everything is stored
The waiterAPICarries orders in and food back

You never walk into the kitchen. You tell the waiter what you want, the kitchen makes it, the waiter brings it back. You have no idea what happened in there — and you do not need to.

That separation is deliberate. It means the kitchen can be rebuilt without touching the dining room; one kitchen can serve several dining rooms — which is exactly what happens here, since RideRescue has a website and a phone app sharing one backend; and a customer cannot reach into the pantry and help themselves.

The real words

  • Frontend — the part on your screen: buttons, maps, text. Also called the client.
  • Backend — a program running on a computer elsewhere that does the thinking and remembers things. Also called the server.
  • Database — an organised store of information. Think filing cabinets.
  • API — the list of things you are allowed to ask the backend to do. Like a menu: you can order what is on it, and nothing else.

03 The three apps

RideRescue is not one program but three, all talking to the same backend.

1. The website

Works in any browser. Used by all four kinds of people, and the only place the admin and shop owners work.

2. The Android app

Installed on a phone, used by customers and mechanics. It exists because a phone can do things a website cannot do well: know your exact position by GPS, and stay open in your hand while you ride.

3. The backend

You never see this one. It runs on the internet day and night, answers questions from both the website and the phone app, and is the only thing allowed to touch the database.

Why not one app?

A website cannot reliably use a phone's GPS while the screen is off, and a phone is a poor place to read a large table of business figures. Each one does what it is good at.

04 The four kinds of people

The project calls these roles. Your role decides what you may see and do.

RoleWhat they do
🧑 CustomerThe rider. Presses the emergency button, books services, tracks the mechanic, buys parts, pays.
🔧 MechanicReceives jobs, travels to the customer, does the repair, gets paid.
🏪 VendorA spare parts shop. Lists parts, manages stock, fulfils orders.
🛡️ AdminRuns the platform. Sees every user, booking and payment, and can block someone.

Everyone uses the same app, and it looks completely different depending on who you are. A customer sees an emergency button; a mechanic sees a list of jobs. That is the role deciding what to show.

05 One complete story

This is the demo. Follow it slowly — it ties everything together. Nawaz's scooter has broken down. Basha is a mechanic about a kilometre away with his app open and marked available.

1

Nawaz presses SOS

He presses and holds the big red button for two seconds. Why hold it? So it cannot be triggered by accident in a pocket — sending a real mechanic to the wrong place wastes their fuel and time.

His phone reads its GPS position — two numbers describing exactly where he is on Earth — and sends them to the backend along with “emergency, this is my bike”.

2

The backend finds the best mechanic

It asks the database: which mechanics are available and within 20 kilometres of this spot? The database answers quickly because it stores locations in a special way built for that question.

Say four come back. Each gets a score out of 1, built from five things:

What is measuredWhy it matters
How far away they areThe biggest factor in an emergency
Their star ratingPast customers' opinion
Whether they are free nowSomeone mid-job will be slower
Years of experienceA harder repair needs a better mechanic
How many jobs they haveDo not overload one person

The highest score wins. In a real booking on the live system, Basha scored 0.7674 and was assigned in under one second. The system also records why, in plain English: “Only 0.4 km away · 5 years experience · Free right now.” A system that shows its reasoning is far easier to defend than one that says “trust me”.

3

Basha's phone lights up

The job appears instantly — not because his phone keeps asking “any jobs? any jobs?”, but because of a socket: think of it as a phone line that stays open, so either side can speak the moment they have something to say. He taps Accept.

4

Nawaz watches him coming

The map shows two markers: a red teardrop pin for Nawaz (marked C) and a blue circle for Basha (marked M). As Basha rides, his phone sends its position every few seconds; the backend works out the distance and arrival time and pushes both to Nawaz's screen. The blue marker moves, the ETA counts down.

Say this before you are asked

The distance is measured in a straight line, not along the roads. Real road distance needs a separate mapping service. It is the obvious next improvement, and naming it yourself looks far better than being caught out.

5

Basha arrives — and proves it is really him

Here is a problem worth thinking about: how do you stop a dishonest mechanic marking a job “done” without doing anything, and charging for it?

The answer: when the job is created the system makes a four-digit code and shows it only to the customer. The mechanic cannot start work until the customer reads it out and he types it in. That single step means he must physically be standing next to the customer. No code, no job, no payment.

6

The work happens, then payment

The bill is calculated on the backend, never on the phone. If the phone worked out the price, someone could tamper with their own phone and pay ₹1 instead of ₹799. Because the backend calculates it, the phone's opinion about price is simply ignored.

Nawaz pays through Razorpay, a real Indian payment company. Razorpay's own secure screen opens, so RideRescue never sees his card details — they are typed into Razorpay's page, not ours.

7

Rating

Nawaz gives Basha a star rating, which feeds back into the scoring in step 2 — so good mechanics get more work over time.

That is the entire flow. Everything else in the app supports it.

06 The technologies, one by one

JavaScript

The programming language everything is written in. Browsers understand it, and it can also run on servers — which is why the project uses one language rather than two.

Node.js

Lets JavaScript run on a server instead of only in a browser. Without it the backend would need a different language.

Express

A helper that makes it easy to say “when someone asks for this, do that”. Those addresses — /api/bookings, /api/payments — are called endpoints. RideRescue has thirteen groups of them.

MongoDB

The database, where information lives permanently. Switch the server off and on and everything is still there. Why this one? Because of the question the app asks constantly — who is near me? — which MongoDB answers directly and quickly.

Socket.IO

The phone line that stays open. Normally the internet works like posting letters: ask a question, get an answer, connection closes. That is useless for live tracking — you would have to keep asking “where is he now?” every three seconds. A socket keeps the line open so the server can speak first. Used here for live location, chat, and notifications.

Next.js and React

What the website is built with. React builds screens out of reusable pieces; Next.js adds page organisation and speed.

React Native and Expo

What the Android app is built with. The clever part: you write the phone app in the same language and style as the website instead of learning a separate one.

Firebase

Google's service, used for one job — sending the OTP, the six-digit code you get by text when signing up.

Why not send the text ourselves?

In India, sending commercial SMS legally requires registering with the telecom regulator as a business, which a student project cannot do. Firebase sidesteps it because Google is the registered sender, not us.

Razorpay

The payment company, used in test mode — free, behaves exactly like the real thing, but no actual money moves.

07 Where information is kept

The database holds twelve collections. A collection is one filing cabinet; each item in it is one document — one person, one booking, one payment.

CollectionWhat is in it
UserEvery person, their role, location and bikes
BookingEvery job: who, which mechanic, status, price
ServiceTypeThe menu of services offered
SparePartEvery item in the shop
OrderEvery parts purchase
PaymentEvery payment attempt, successful or not
ReviewStar ratings customers leave
MessageChat between customer and mechanic
NotificationAlerts in the bell icon
ComplaintProblems raised for the admin
CouponDiscount codes
OtpTemporary sign-up codes, auto-deleted when expired

The one clever bit

An index is like the index at the back of a textbook. Without it, finding a topic means reading every page; with it you jump straight there.

RideRescue puts a special index on people's locations, called a 2dsphere index. “2dsphere” means it understands the Earth is a ball, not a flat sheet, so distances come out right. This is what makes “find available mechanics within 20 km, nearest first” fast even with many users.

The classic mistake

Locations are stored as [longitude, latitude] — in that order. Everyone says “latitude and longitude” out loud, so the reversed order catches people constantly. Get it backwards and your mechanic appears in the sea.

08 The “AI”, honestly

The honest framing

These are not neural networks. They are scoring systems built from clear rules — and that was a deliberate choice.

Why rules instead of a learned model?

  1. A learned model needs training data. Teaching a computer to pick good mechanics would need thousands of past bookings with known outcomes. A new project has none.
  2. You can explain a rule. The app can say “chosen because 0.4 km away and free right now”. A neural network cannot tell you why it decided anything.
  3. It works from day one, with no waiting and no data collection.

Saying this yourself is far stronger than being caught out by it.

The four features

1. Mechanic matching — the scoring from step 2. The weights change for an emergency: distance jumps from 32% to 50% of the decision, because when you are stranded, who arrives soonest matters more than who has the best reviews.

2. Parts recommendation — suggests parts that fit your specific bike, based on your make and model and what similar bikes have needed.

3. Predictive maintenance — predicts when each part is due, using two measures and taking whichever comes first: distance ridden and time passed. That mirrors how manufacturers write real service schedules — “every 3,000 km or 6 months, whichever is sooner”.

4. Chatbot — works out which of nine topics you mean, then looks up your real data. Ask “where is my mechanic” and it gives the actual current ETA, not a canned reply.

09 How it stays safe

“Is it secure?” is a guaranteed question, so each of these is worth understanding.

Passwords are never stored. Your password goes through a one-way scrambler called bcrypt and only the scrambled result is kept. Scrambling is one-way — it cannot be reversed. At login your typed password is scrambled again and the two are compared. Even someone who steals the whole database cannot read anyone's password.

Logging in gives you a token. The server hands your app a signed pass called a JWT, shown with every later request — like a wristband at a festival. It is signed so it cannot be forged, and it expires after seven days.

Your role limits what you can reach. A customer's token simply cannot open the admin pages, and the server checks on every single request.

The best point to make

Having the right role is not enough. A customer cannot read another customer's booking, even though both are customers — the server also checks the booking is actually yours. That distinction, role versus ownership, is the strongest security point in the project.

Your phone number is proved, not claimed. Anyone can type any number into a form, so the app never trusts it. Firebase sends the code, checks it, and returns a signed certificate that the number is yours; the backend verifies that signature before creating the account.

Payments are verified with maths. When Razorpay reports “this was paid” it includes a signature that could only be produced by someone holding our secret key. The backend recalculates it and compares. A fake “I paid, honest” message from a phone is rejected.

Prices are always recalculated on the server, so the phone's opinion about money is ignored entirely.

Being honest about the weaknesses

Naming your own limitations makes you look like an engineer; being caught out by them does the opposite.

  • The login token is stored where the browser's JavaScript can read it. A stricter method exists but complicates the phone app.
  • There is no limit on login attempts, so someone could keep guessing passwords. A standard tool fixes this and would be the first thing to add.

11 Answers to likely questions

What is this project?
An app that connects a stranded two-wheeler rider with the nearest available mechanic, tracks that mechanic live on a map, and sells spare parts alongside it.
Who uses it?
Four kinds of people — customers, mechanics, spare parts vendors and an administrator. Each sees a completely different app.
How does it find the nearest mechanic?
The database stores everyone's position with a special index for location questions, so it can quickly find mechanics within 20 km. Those are then scored on distance, rating, availability, experience and current workload, and the best one is assigned.
How does live tracking work?
The mechanic's phone sends its position every few seconds over a connection that stays open, so the customer's map updates immediately rather than the app having to keep asking.
Is it really AI?
It is an explainable scoring system rather than a neural network — deliberately. It can justify every decision in words and works without training data. A learned model would be the next step once there are enough completed bookings to learn from.
Is it secure?
Passwords are scrambled one-way and never stored. Logging in gives a signed token that expires. Your role limits what you can reach, and separately, you can only see records that are actually yours. Payments are verified by a cryptographic signature and prices are always recalculated on the server.
What would you improve?
Real road distances instead of straight lines, a limit on login attempts, and a learned ranking model once there is enough real booking data to train one.

If you remember nothing else:

The phone and the website are just screens. The backend does the thinking and the database does the remembering. Everything the app does is one of those three, talking to the other two.
